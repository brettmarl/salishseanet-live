Salish Sea AIS Network

Open vessel data
for the Salish Sea.

Salish Seanet is a network of small receiving stations hosted by local volunteers around the Salish Sea. Each station listens for the position reports that ships and boats broadcast over marine VHF radio (AIS) and shares them for public use in maps, apps, research, and marine safety.

How it works Host a Lookout

How it works

Vessels already announce where they are. A Salish Seanet station hears them and passes that information on.

Vessels broadcast

Ships, ferries, tugs and many recreational boats carry AIS transponders that broadcast their identity, position, course and speed over marine VHF, every few seconds to a few minutes.

A Lookout listens

A Seanet Lookout, a small low-power box with a VHF antenna, picks up those broadcasts. It only listens. It never transmits on the marine band.

Open vessel data

The station checks each message and forwards it over the host's internet connection to AIS data networks such as AIS Hub and VesselFinder, where it feeds public maps, apps, and research.

Why more Lookouts matter

AIS travels by line of sight. Islands, headlands and narrow channels leave radio shadows that no single big station can see around. Many small Lookouts, spread around the shoreline, fill in the gaps.

Marine safety

Better coverage means a more complete picture of traffic for mariners, search and rescue, and anyone keeping watch.

Research

Vessel traffic data supports work on shipping patterns, underwater noise, and how boats share the water with marine life.

Maps & apps

The same data powers the public vessel maps and apps that boaters, ferry riders and ship-spotters use every day.

Hosted by local volunteers.

Every station is provided by a community host who contributes a location, power, and internet access to improve vessel tracking in the Salish Sea. Find out what hosting involves →

The Seanet Lookout

A complete, weatherproof AIS receiving station for your shoreline. Built to be small, quiet and hands-off, so it can run unattended for years.

Best-in-class receiver

The high-performance dAISy-catcher, tuned for maximum range and listening on both AIS frequencies at once.

Marine VHF antenna

Mounted as high as the site allows. The higher the antenna, the farther the station can hear.

Low power

Runs on a couple of watts, from a single network cable (Power over Ethernet) or a USB power adapter.

Privacy & the data

  • AIS is public by design. Vessels broadcast it openly so that other vessels can see them and avoid collisions.
  • The station only listens to marine VHF. It has no camera or microphone, and it does not look at or collect anything else on the host's network.
  • What it sends: the AIS messages it receives, plus a small periodic status check-in (firmware version, uptime, Wi-Fi signal strength and message counts) so we can tell it's healthy.
  • Station location: AIS data networks ask for each station's approximate position, so they know the area it covers. Hosts' names and addresses are never published.

Questions

What is AIS?

The Automatic Identification System. Ships and many smaller boats carry an AIS transponder that broadcasts the vessel's name, position, course and speed on two marine VHF channels near 162 MHz. Anyone with a receiver can hear it.

Does the station transmit anything over the radio?

No. It only receives. All it sends goes over the host's internet connection.

How much does it cost to power?

About $3 a year. It draws about two watts, less than a single LED light bulb.

Will it slow down my internet?

No. It uses a tiny bit of upload spread through the day, about the same as loading a few web pages, and doesn't touch your download speed.

Is it safe on my network?

Yes. It only sends AIS data out, accepts no incoming connections, and never talks to your other devices. More for hosts →

What happens if the power or internet goes out?

Nothing to do. The station starts again on its own when power returns and picks up where it left off when the connection comes back.

Where can I see the data?

On public vessel-tracking maps such as VesselFinder. AIS Hub also shares the combined data with organizations that contribute their own feeds.

Can I host a Lookout?

We'd love to hear from you, especially if you have a spot with a clear view of the water. See hosting a station.
